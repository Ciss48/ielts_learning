Matching-headings questions test one thing: whether you can find the controlling idea of a paragraph without reading every word. Examiners write distractors around details that appear in the paragraph but do not govern it.

The three-pass method

  1. Read the headings list first and group them: two or three will be near-synonyms — mark those pairs.
  2. Skim each paragraph's first and last sentence, then the topic-shift word (however, in contrast, as a result).
  3. Assign confident matches first. Leave hard paragraphs for the second pass, when fewer headings remain.
A heading describes what the paragraph does, not what it mentions. If you can only justify a match with one sentence, it is probably a distractor.

Timing target

Give this task type 90 seconds per paragraph maximum. In the practice step you will run a 6-paragraph passage against a 9-minute clock.

  • Never re-read a paragraph you have already matched.
  • Write the heading number in the margin, not in the box, until the pass is done.

The quiet return of the urban tram

AFor most of the twentieth century the tram was treated as an obstacle. Cities from Manchester to Melbourne tore up rails to make room for private cars, and by 1970 the vehicle survived mainly in tourist photographs.

BThe reversal began not with nostalgia but with arithmetic. Planners comparing the cost per passenger-kilometre of buses, metros and light rail found that trams occupied an unexpectedly efficient middle ground, particularly on corridors carrying between three and eight thousand riders an hour.

CHowever, the arithmetic alone did not persuade councils. What changed the argument was evidence from Freiburg and Strasbourg that a tram line reshapes the street around it — widening pavements, slowing traffic and, in several documented cases, raising retail turnover along the route.
